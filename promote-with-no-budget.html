The short answer

To promote a startup with no marketing budget, you trade money for time: fix one clear sentence about who the product is for, spend Week 1 on that message and on free directory listings, Week 2 on launch posts and communities, Week 3 on cross-promotion with neighbouring makers, and Week 4 doubling the one channel that actually moved. Nothing here can guarantee a single signup — what it gives you is a fair test, in the right order, at zero spend.

The part nobody warns you about

You finished the product. So why does nobody know it exists?

Building felt like the hard part because it had a finish line. Promotion has none — and the standard advice was written for teams with money. If any of this sounds familiar, the problem is not your product. It is the order you were told to do things in.

1

The launch post went out — and the silence came back

You posted once, in a few places, on the day you finished. A handful of friends reacted kindly, a few strangers clicked, and then nothing. That is not a failed product; that is one post doing the work of a whole month of promotion. A single announcement reaches only the people already watching you, and at the start almost nobody is. Founders who share their first weeks in public say the same thing in different words: the first users came from repeated, personal effort — answering questions, writing to people one at a time, showing up again the next day — not from the announcement itself.

2

Every marketing article quietly assumes a budget

Read the popular guides closely and the budget is hidden in plain sight: run a test campaign, hire someone to write, sponsor a newsletter, boost the post that performs. When your budget is ₹0 or $0, those steps are not advice — they are a wall. What is left once you remove every paid step is shorter and more honest than most guides admit: your message, your time in the places your buyers already gather, listings that stay up, and other makers willing to trade attention fairly. This page is built from only those four things.

3

Your page cannot explain itself in one sentence yet

Here is a test that costs nothing and hurts a little: ask someone who has never seen your product to read your homepage for ten seconds and say what it does and who it is for. If they hesitate, promotion is not your first problem — the message is. Every free channel sends people to that one page, and a confused page wastes free attention exactly as efficiently as it wastes paid attention. Fixing that sentence is the highest-value hour in this entire plan, which is why it sits in Week 1, before any posting.

4

You are measuring the wrong thing, so everything looks dead

At zero budget, follower counts and page views will lie to you for weeks. The numbers that tell the truth early are replies, conversations, and signups from a named source: this person found you in that community, that listing sent this visit, that partner mentioned you and three people tried the product. Keep a plain log — a notebook page or a spreadsheet with a name, a source and a date — from day one. Without it, Week 4 of this plan, where you double down on what worked, is impossible, because you will not know what worked.

The zero-budget mindset

Money is replaced by time and honesty — spend both like they matter

There are no free channels, only channels you pay for with hours instead of money. One founder reply, quoted in a recent no-budget marketing breakdown, put it bluntly: you spend your own time, and time is money too. So budget your hours the way a funded team budgets cash — and notice where an hour converts into a real conversation, and where it evaporates.

1

Hours convert where a person already has the problem

The fastest honest return on an hour is a conversation with someone who described your exact problem in public, recently, in their own words — a forum question, a community post, a comment asking whether a tool like yours exists. You are not interrupting them; you are answering. Ten researched, personal messages beat a hundred copied ones, and a full, useful answer in a community thread keeps being found by people with the same problem long after you wrote it. This is why direct conversation sits at the top of the speed ranking further down this page.

2

Hours evaporate where you are performing, not helping

Posting the same link in place after place, polishing a profile nobody visits, rewriting a bio for the fifth time, checking analytics every hour — all of it feels like marketing and produces almost none. A useful rule: if an hour of work would look identical whether your product was good or bad, it is performance, not promotion. Real promotion is specific — it names a person, a problem, or a place — and it leaves evidence behind: a reply, a listing, a page, a relationship.

3

One channel, done properly, beats five done thinly

Every serious no-budget guide lands on the same instruction: pick one audience and one main channel, run it long enough to judge it, and only then add the next. Ten minutes a day scattered across five platforms produces five weak signals and no decision you can trust. The playbook below still covers several channels across four weeks — but in sequence, each with a job and a finish line, so by the end of the month you know which one deserves your next month.

4

Honesty is not a style choice — it is the whole asset

With no budget, your reputation is your distribution. Communities remember who helped and who spammed. Buyers remember who described a product plainly and who oversold it. Say what your product does, say what it does not do yet, disclose that it is yours when you mention it, and never dress up a guess as a result. It reads slower. It compounds faster. And it is the one advantage a broke founder holds over a funded competitor: people can tell the difference, and they root for the honest one in public, where other buyers can see it.

The playbook — week by week

How to promote your product for free in 4 weeks [with hours]

Four weeks, roughly ten focused hours a week, zero spend. Each week has one job. If you have fewer hours, stretch each week over two — the order matters more than the speed. For the wider map of places worth your time, start with where to promote your startup, place by place, then work this plan in order.

W1

Week 1: Fix the message, then list in free directories (about 8 hours)

The message — 3 hours. Write one sentence that says who the product is for, what it does, and what changes for them. Test it on your homepage headline, then ask three people who match your buyer to read the page and repeat the sentence back in their own words. Rewrite until they can. The proof — 2 hours. Record one short clip or take three honest screenshots of the product doing its main job; every launch, listing and post in the next three weeks will reuse them. The listings — 3 hours. Write your directory text once, properly — what it is, who it is for, how it works — then submit to the relevant free directories and launch boards in your category, not to long lists of unrelated ones. Keep a log of where you submitted and when each listing goes live.

W2

Week 2: Launch posts and communities (about 10 hours)

Prepare one launch — 3 hours. Choose the one launch board that fits your product best and prepare it properly: the Week 1 sentence as the tagline, the screenshots, the short clip, and you present on the day to answer every comment yourself. Communities — 5 hours. Join two or three communities where your buyers already ask questions. Read the rules first, spend the first days answering questions in full with no link at all, then publish one honest post about the problem you built for, mentioning the product as one answer. Our deeper guide to promoting on Reddit without getting removed covers the rules that matter most there. Direct messages — 2 hours. Write personally to ten people who described the problem in public in the last week. Short, specific, no copied pitch — and log every reply.

W3

Week 3: Cross-promotion and partnerships (about 9 hours)

Find partners — 3 hours. List makers whose products serve the same audience as yours without competing with you: a neighbouring tool, a newsletter in your niche, a creator your buyers already follow. Make a fair offer — 3 hours. Propose a specific swap, not a vague favour: you feature their project in your next post or inside your product, they feature yours; you write a joint guide; you review each other honestly in public. The full method is in our guide to cross-promotion between makers. Follow up — 3 hours. Return to every Week 2 conversation that went quiet with one useful follow-up — a fix you shipped, an answer you promised — then stop. Chasing people past two follow-ups spends the hours Week 4 needs.

W4

Week 4: Double down on whatever moved (about 8 hours)

Count honestly — 2 hours. Open the log you kept from day one and count, by source: replies, visits you can attribute, and signups. Expect the picture to be uneven — one channel will have done most of the work, and at least one will have done nothing. That is the information the whole month was for. Cut and concentrate — 4 hours. Drop the weakest channel without regret and put its hours into the strongest one: more answers in the community that replied, a second partnership like the one that worked, a better listing where the clicks came from. Set the next target — 2 hours. If your goal is the early-user milestone most founders aim at first, our guide to getting your first 100 users shows how the channels in this plan stack towards it — slowly, from real conversations, not from one lucky spike.

Free channels, ranked by speed

Which free channel pays back first? Fastest to slowest

Speed is not the same as value. The fastest channels below fade quickly; the slowest one keeps paying after you stop. A sane zero-budget month uses the fast ones to learn and the slow ones to last — in that order.

1

Communities and direct conversation — fastest

Answering a real question, or writing personally to someone who just described the problem, can bring a reply the same day. What it can actually bring: your first real conversations, your sharpest feedback, and early users who feel personally invited — in ones and twos, not in crowds. It stops the moment you stop, and it only works while you are genuinely useful; lead with help, disclose the product is yours, and respect each community’s rules on self-promotion.

2

Launch boards — a fast spike, once

A launch board puts a new product in front of people who came that day to find new products. Prepare once, launch once, and be present for every comment. What it can actually bring: a concentrated burst of curious visitors and honest first impressions over a day or two — then it ends. Treat a launch as a test of your message in front of strangers, not as a supply of users, and never burn your one good launch on an unfinished page.

3

Directories — slow to start, steady after

A relevant directory listing takes under an hour, stays up, and is found by people comparing options in your category. What it can actually bring: a thin, steady trickle of well-matched visitors over months, plus a page about your product that you do not have to maintain. It will not bring a crowd, a free queue on a popular directory can be slow, and an irrelevant directory brings nothing at all — relevance is the whole game.

4

The discovery circle — built for this exact problem

A mutual-discovery circle exists because every channel above asks a founder to find an audience alone. Inside a circle, members discover each other’s projects as part of how the platform works. What it can actually bring: real discovery by fellow builders who understand what a new project needs — honest attention from people in the same position as you. GrowTogether is one such circle, and it is covered honestly in its own section below: it is at waitlist stage, so it belongs in your plan as a channel that opens, not one you can use today.

5

Search content — slowest, but it compounds

Write the page that answers the question your buyer types into a search engine — the problem, the comparison, the how-to — better and more honestly than the pages already there. What it can actually bring: nothing this week, and possibly nothing this month; then, if the page earns its place, visitors who arrive while you sleep, for as long as the page stays useful. Publishing does not ensure ranking, and thin pages written only to rank tend to stay invisible. One genuinely useful page beats a pile of quick ones.

Protect your hours

What NOT to waste time on [the time-traps]

Most zero-budget advice fails not on what it recommends but on what it forgets to warn against. These are the traps that came up again and again in the founder stories and honest guides behind this page — each one feels like progress while it eats the only budget you have.

1

Mass directory spam: submitting everywhere, relevant or not

There are long lists of directories that accept anything, and submitting to all of them can fill a satisfying afternoon. It is still a trap. An irrelevant directory sends no buyers, a listing nobody in your category reads is not distribution, and the hours come straight out of the conversations that actually convert. Submit to the directories your buyers genuinely use — a focused set, written properly once — and stop there. Volume is not a strategy; it is a way to feel busy.

2

Buying followers, likes, or reviews

Bought followers are accounts that will never buy, never reply, and never recommend you — and platforms read a crowd that never engages as a signal to show your posts to fewer real people. Bought reviews are worse: marketplaces and review sites work to detect them, listings get removed, and real buyers spot the pattern in seconds. Worst of all, fake numbers destroy the one tool this plan runs on — your log — because you can no longer tell what actually worked.

3

Dropping your link in random groups

Posting the same promotional message across groups and communities you have never contributed to is the fastest way to be removed, muted, or quietly banned from the exact places your buyers gather. Moderators and members can tell a drive-by link from a participant in one glance. The honest version is slower — read the rules, answer questions first, post where sharing your own work is welcome — and it is the only version that leaves the door open for your next post.

4

SEO content farms: many thin pages, written for engines

Publishing a pile of short, near-identical pages stuffed with repeated phrases is an old tactic that search engines have spent years learning to ignore — and readers learned to leave within seconds. It also burns the hours your one good page needed. Write fewer pages, each answering one real question thoroughly, in the words your buyers actually use. If a page would embarrass you when read aloud to a customer, it will not rank for long either.

5

Follow-for-follow and engagement swaps with strangers

Trading follows with people who have no interest in your problem inflates a number while teaching every platform that your audience does not care about your posts — which lowers how far the real ones travel. The same applies to copied comments and empty engagement on strangers’ posts. Spend that hour instead on one thoughtful comment where your buyers actually are; a single real conversation is worth more than a wall of hollow numbers, and everyone watching can tell the difference.

Questions people search for

No-budget promotion, answered honestly

How can I promote a startup with no budget?

Replace money with time and honesty. Fix one clear sentence that says who the product is for and what changes for them, tell people who already have the problem one at a time, answer questions in the communities they already use, list the product in relevant free directories, launch once in a place built for launches, and swap promotion with a neighbouring maker. Do one channel properly before adding the next one.

How do I promote my product for free in the first month?

Week 1, fix the message and list the product in free directories. Week 2, publish launch posts and join the communities where your buyers already talk. Week 3, trade cross-promotion with makers serving the same audience. Week 4, count replies, visits and signups by source, drop the weakest channel and put the saved hours into the one that moved. The plan on this page gives the hours for each task.

What free channel works fastest when you have no audience?

Direct conversation is fastest. A personal message to someone who described the problem in public, or a full answer to their question in a community, can bring a reply the same day. Launch boards are next, because they put a new product in front of people already looking for one. Directories and search content are slower, but a listing or a useful page keeps working after you stop working on it.

Is directory submission worth it if I have no marketing budget?

Yes, if the directory is relevant and the listing is written properly once. A listing costs an hour or less, stays up, and can be found by people comparing tools in your category. It is not worth submitting to long lists of unrelated directories. Relevance beats volume, and a listing on its own will not bring a crowd. Treat it as a signpost that supports the faster channels, not as the plan itself.
