Cross-promotion: how projects promote each other — for free

Cross-promotion is the oldest free growth tactic there is: two projects with overlapping audiences recommend each other, and both grow without buying ads. The short answer

Find projects near your size whose audience would genuinely care about yours, agree one exact exchange — who recommends what, where, and in which week — and run it with a trackable link. Then report back honestly and repeat with the partners who delivered. Vague shoutouts between mismatched audiences waste both sides.

What cross-promotion is

The forms cross-promotion takes

People also call it mutual promotion or reciprocal promotion. Whatever the name, the shape is the same — your audience hears about them, their audience hears about you. It takes four common forms — plus a fifth, newer one that is still being built:

1

Shoutout swaps

Two channels, accounts or pages recommend each other in a post. The classic social swap. The honest limit: one post, one spike, then it is gone — and it only works if both sides actually publish.

2

Recommendation swaps

Newsletters feature each other in an issue; podcasters swap trailers; blogs exchange guest posts. Slower than a shoutout, but the endorsement is deeper. The honest limit: arranging them one by one, by email, eats hours every week. Our newsletter promotion guide and podcast promotion guide teach the manual version in detail.

3

Recommendation networks

Some platforms automate recommendations between their own users — Substack and beehiiv suggest similar newsletters to each publication’s readers. The honest limit: you must publish on that platform to take part, and the boosted placements inside them are paid.

4

Bundles and shared lists

Several projects packaged into one link — a shared Telegram folder, a joint resource page, a bundle of tools. One member’s audience discovers all of them at once. The honest limit: someone has to organise the bundle, and the organiser usually takes the biggest share of attention.

5

Discovery circles

The newest form — and the one this site is building: members join one circle, discover each other’s projects as a built-in habit, and earn discovery back in a fair rotation. No partner-hunting, no renegotiating. The honest limit: a circle only works once enough genuine members are in it — which is why circles start with a waitlist and a reviewed beta.

Why the manual version breaks

One-off swaps fail in predictable ways

Cross-promotion itself works — it is how most projects find their first audiences. What fails is running it by hand, forever, with strangers. Three failure modes show up every time:

1

The partner hunt

Finding projects near your size, in your topic, that reply to messages and keep their word takes longer than the promotion itself. Most swaps die in the inbox before they start.

2

The fairness problem

One side has ten times the audience, or buries the mention, or never publishes at all. With no memory of who did what, every swap is a fresh act of trust — and trust runs out.

3

The one-type wall

Most swap communities serve a single project type — newsletters swap with newsletters, apps with apps. But your next thousand users might read a newsletter, watch a channel and use an app. A website has nothing to trade with any of them.

A cross-promotion platform exists to remove exactly these three problems: it holds the member list, matches by size and topic, remembers who promoted whom, and — in GrowTogether’s case — lets every project type trade attention with every other. Do it by hand, fairly

How to run a cross-promotion swap that works

Not ready for a platform? A manual swap done properly still beats any ad a beginner can afford. The difference between swaps that work and swaps that waste a week is fairness, agreed in advance:

1

Match audience first, size second

Pick partners whose audience would genuinely care about your project, at roughly your size — within about double in either direction. A modest, relevant audience sends people who stay; a large, wrong one sends a bounce.

2

Agree the exact exchange

What gets published, where it appears, on what date, for how long. A dedicated recommendation beats a buried link every time — say which one you are trading before either side publishes.

3

Use a trackable link

Give your partner a unique link — an invite link, a tagged URL, a dedicated page — so both sides can see what the swap actually sent. Untracked swaps run on vibes, and vibes end partnerships.

4

Report back, honestly

Share your real numbers afterwards, even when they disappoint. The partners who report honestly get repeat swaps; the ones who inflate get quietly dropped by everyone.

5

Repeat with the winners

Two or three reliable partners, swapping on a rhythm, beat twenty one-off deals. This is also the point where most people hand the whole routine to a platform — it is exactly the loop a circle automates.

The ask itself

A cross-promotion pitch you can copy

Most swaps die in the inbox because the message asks for everything and offers nothing specific. This shape gets replies — short, concrete, and easy to say yes to. Replace the brackets, keep the honesty:

Subject: A straight swap for our two [topics]?

Hi [name] — I run [project]: [one line — what it is, and who it is for]. [Honest size: "about 300 readers" or "just started — roughly 40 subscribers"].

Your [project] reaches the same people from the other side, so here is a straight swap: this week I feature [their project] in [my next issue / a post / my recommendations page], and you feature [my project] in yours — same prominence, same week, and we each send the other the link when it is live.

If the fit is wrong, no hard feelings at all. And if you want to look first, my project is here: [link].

Three rules make it work: name the exact exchange, name the week, and make declining cost nothing. The vague asks — "let’s collaborate sometime" — are the ones that die.

Questions people search for

Cross-promotion, answered honestly

What is cross-promotion?

Two or more projects with overlapping audiences recommend each other to their own audiences — a newsletter featuring another newsletter, two channels swapping shoutouts, an app linking to a partner app. Nobody buys ads; each side lends the other its audience, and both grow. It is also called mutual promotion or reciprocal promotion.

What is the difference between a swap and a cross-promotion platform?

A swap is a one-off deal you arrange yourself: find a partner, agree terms, publish, hope they do the same. A platform organises the matching, the fairness and the memory — who promoted whom, who is owed a turn — so the exchange keeps running without weekly renegotiation.

Are cross-promotion platforms free?

Some are. Substack and beehiiv include recommendation features for publications hosted on them, though beehiiv’s boosted placements are paid. CrossPromo Club and Lettergrowth help newsletters find swap partners for free. How do I find cross-promotion partners?

Look for projects near your size whose audience would genuinely care about yours. Niche directories, category communities and cross-promotion platforms all list potential partners. Match on audience fit first and size second — a modest, relevant audience converts better than a large, wrong one.
