First users — the honest sequence

How to get your first 100 users — without a budget

Here is the truth nobody selling growth wants to say: your first 100 users are not a traffic problem. They are one hundred individual conversations. This guide gives you the order they actually arrive in — who to ask first, where to show up next, and which moves to save for later.

The short answer

Ask people who know you, one by one — then show up in the communities where your users gather, contributing before you share. List the project in directories, concentrate everything on one launch moment, swap promotion with projects your size, and give your first users an invite link.

The sequence — in the order that works

First 100 users, stage by stage

The stages overlap — real projects run two at once. But the order matters: each stage makes the next one easier, and skipping to the later stages first is the most common way beginners waste months.

1

Users 1–10: ask people who already know you

Write to real people, one at a time: contacts, past colleagues, followers on any profile you already have, members of groups you belong to. Say what you built, who it is for, and ask them to try it — not to “support” you. The honest limit: these users are kind, and kind feedback lies. Watch what they do, not what they say. Y Combinator’s famous advice for exactly this stage: do things that don’t scale.

2

Users 10–30: show up where your users already gather

Find the rooms: the subreddit, the Discord server, the Facebook group, the forum, Indie Hackers for builders. Join as a member first. Read the room’s own rules before anything else — many subreddits ban self-promotion outright, and most have karma and account-age gates a new account cannot pass. Answer questions and be useful first, for weeks if that is what the room expects — then share what you built, in the way that community allows, saying plainly that it is yours. The honest limit: communities smell drive-by promotion instantly, and bans are forever. Contribution first is not politeness; it is the mechanism.

3

Users 25–50: get listed, so discovery keeps dripping

Submit your project to the directories and listing sites in your category. A listing is slow — a few visitors a day, sometimes less — but it works while you sleep and it never sends you a bill. Start with our free advertising sites directory and the startup places directory. The honest limit: listings compound over months, not days. Plant them early precisely because they are slow.

4

Users 40–70: create one launch moment, and swap

Pick one stage — a launch platform, a community launch day, a Show HN post — and concentrate everything on it: your list, your communities, your listings all pointing at one day. In the same weeks, swap promotion with two or three projects near your size, the way our cross-promotion guide explains. The honest limit: a launch is a spike, not a staircase. What matters is how many of the spike’s visitors you keep.

5

Users 60–100: let the first users bring the next ones

Give every user a personal invite link and a reason to share it — inside GrowTogether, members who bring genuine builders through their link enter the beta first, and the same mechanic works anywhere. Then double the one route from stages 1–4 that actually sent people, and cut the rest. The honest limit: referrals only work if the first users genuinely got value. No invite mechanic rescues a project people don’t return to.

6

What never works: buying the hundred

Bought users are fake or paid-to-pretend accounts. They never return, they teach you nothing, and they corrupt every decision you make afterwards — you cannot tell whether your project works when the audience was hired to applaud. One hundred people who chose you are the asset. There is no shortcut version of them.

The words themselves

Two first messages you can copy

Advice fails at the blank page: you know you should “reach out” and “post in communities”, and then you stare at an empty box. Here are the two messages that start almost every project’s first hundred — written the way this guide believes in: honest, labelled, and easy to say no to. Fill every bracket with something true, or do not send it.

A

The community post opener

For the room where your users gather — posted only where the rules allow makers to share, after you have contributed there first (stage 2 above). The structure matters more than the words: label first, story second, ask last.

[One line: what it is] — I built a free [thing] that [does the one job], because [the real problem, in one honest line].

Full disclosure: I’m the person who built it, so this is self-promotion with the label on. I kept hitting [the problem] while [doing the thing this community does], and the options I found all [the gap you kept hitting]. [Name] is my attempt: it [the one thing it does well], and it deliberately does not [the thing people will ask about first].

I’d rather hear what’s wrong with it than collect praise. If you try it, tell me the first thing that annoyed you. Link: [link]. Mods — if this breaks the rules, remove it and tell me where it should have gone.

Why it works: the disclosure disarms the room, the gap gives members something to judge, and the ask — “what annoyed you” — is one people enjoy answering. Stay in the thread and answer every reply the same day. And never paste the same text into five rooms: communities compare notes, and a tour is visible from space.

B

The direct message / email opener

For one person at a time — someone who knows you, or who publicly described the exact problem (stage 1). This is the fastest free route there is, and the easiest to ruin by sending it to a list.

Hi [name] — you [said / wrote / asked] [the specific thing] in [the place] last week, and it stuck with me, because it is exactly the problem I built [name] for.

It is [one line: what it does], it is free, and it is live here: [link]. Would you try it for [the specific job they described] and tell me honestly whether it misses?

If it is not for you, that is useful too — and I will not chase you either way. — [your name]

The first line is the whole message: it must be true, specific, and impossible to send to anyone else. If you cannot write it, you do not know this person well enough to message them yet — go and learn first. Send twenty of these over two weeks, not two hundred in a day. A no, and even silence, is information; arguing with either is how a founder becomes a spammer.

The sequence, as a checklist

Your first 100 users, week by week

The stages above, turned into boxes you can tick. Four honest weeks — most projects take longer, and the checklist simply repeats at the next stage’s scale.

1

Week 1 — Ask (users 1–10)

  • ☐ Write down 20 people who know you and plausibly need this
  • ☐ Message 10 of them personally — one by one, with the opener above
  • ☐ Ask every person who tries it one question: “What nearly stopped you?”
  • ☐ Fix the top complaint before recruiting a single new person
  • ☐ Start the habit that never stops: write down where each user came from
2

Week 2 — Show up (users 10–30)

  • ☐ Pick three communities where your users already gather
  • ☐ Read each one’s rules — note the karma and account-age gates and the self-promotion policy
  • ☐ Answer five real questions in each, saying nothing about your project
  • ☐ Share the project once, where the rules allow it, with the community opener
  • ☐ Answer every reply the same day — the thread is the promotion
3

Week 3 — Get listed, prepare the moment (users 25–50)

  • ☐ Submit to the directories in your category — start with our free advertising sites directory
  • ☐ Give each listing its own link, so you can see what each one actually sends
  • ☐ Pick the launch day and the one stage it happens on
  • ☐ Line up two or three swap partners near your size, with the exact exchange agreed
  • ☐ Tell your first users the launch date — personally, not in a broadcast
4

Week 4 — Launch, then invite (users 50–100)

  • ☐ Launch day: every channel you have points at the one stage
  • ☐ Reply to everything, all day — speed is the whole launch-day skill
  • ☐ Give every user a personal invite link and one honest reason to share it
  • ☐ Write down which single route sent the most real users
  • ☐ Next month: double that route, and cut the rest without sentiment

When it stalls — because it will

What to do when a stage stops working

Every project stalls somewhere in the hundred. The stall is information: each stage fails in its own way, and each failure has a specific fix that is never “do more of everything”.

1

Nobody you ask says yes

Then the problem is the offer, not the outreach — people who know you are the warmest audience you will ever have. Ask two of the noes one honest question — “what would have to be true for this to be worth your time?” — and believe the answer. Fix the thing, then ask again. More messages to more strangers will not repair a message the warmest room already refused.

2

Communities ignore or remove your post

Removal means the room read the post as an ad — and the room is usually right. Go back to contributing for another stretch, longer than feels fair, and study the posts the room rewards: the shape of them is the shape yours has to earn. If three honest attempts in three different rooms all fail the same way, the project’s one-line description is the suspect — rewrite it until a stranger can repeat it back to you.

3

Listings send almost nothing

That is their normal first month, not a verdict. Check the two things that are actually in your control: is the listing complete — every field, real images, the words a searcher types — and does the page it sends people to answer the promise the listing made? A listing pointing at a page that talks about something else converts at zero forever, and no second listing fixes it.

4

The launch spikes, then flatlines

The spike was never the asset; the kept fraction is. Count how many of the launch-day users returned within a week, and interview two of the ones who did not — their reason is your next month’s work. A second launch to the same audience will not save a project the first audience tried and left; a fixed reason, honestly announced to the same people, sometimes does.

5

The shape of the hundred, honestly

For most projects the hundred does not arrive in the stage order printed on this page — it arrives lumpy: ten from people you know, a slow twenty from rooms and listings, a lump from one good day, and the rest from the users themselves. The stages are not a staircase you climb once; they are four engines you keep running, at the weight each one has earned. When the total stalls, the question is never “how do I get traffic?” — it is “which engine stopped, and what did it tell me last time it ran?”

One last habit, worth more than any single stage: keep the list. Every person who tried the project, where they came from, and what they said — written down from user one. By user fifty that list is your launch audience, your swap Rolodex and your memory of what actually worked, and it cannot be rebuilt later at any price.

Questions people search for

First 100 users, answered honestly

How do I get my first 100 users for free?

In this order: ask people who already know you, personally and one by one. Then show up in the communities where your users already gather, contributing before you share. Then list your project in directories so discovery keeps dripping in. Then create one launch moment, swap promotion with similar projects, and give your first users an easy way to invite the next ones.

How long does it take to get the first 100 users?

For most projects working at it daily, weeks to a few months. The first 10 usually take longer than the next 90, because every real user makes the next one easier to convince. Anyone promising 100 users in a weekend is selling something — usually fake accounts.

What is the fastest free way to get first users?

Direct, personal outreach to people who already know you or already asked for what you built — messages written for one person at a time. It does not scale, and that is exactly why it works at the start.

Should I buy my first 100 users?

No. Bought users are typically fake or incentivised accounts that never return, and they poison every number you would learn from. One hundred real users who chose you are worth more than ten thousand bought ones.
