Product Hunt alternatives: 8 sites like Product Hunt with a free route

Product Hunt gives a product one day in the light, and most products do not win that day. These Product Hunt alternatives spread the launch across more days and more boards — and every one of the 8 below was checked on its own site, with its real cost and its real catch stated plainly. No board on this page is presented as free when it is not.

The short answer

The best Product Hunt alternative depends on your stage. Still building? Use a waitlist-friendly board and join the free queues early, because most free lanes are slow. Launching this week? Pick boards where you choose the date or where the listing stays up for days, not hours. Already launched? Use the boards that keep a permanent page and keep sending readers over time. The 8 boards below all have a real free or freemium route today; two famous names that no longer do are named separately, so you do not waste a submission on them.

Why makers look further

Why people look beyond Product Hunt

Product Hunt is not broken, and this page is not an argument against it. It is the reference point for a reason: a strong community, a clear daily ritual, and a launch day that forces a maker to finish the listing, the screenshots and the story. The reasons to look beyond it are structural, and they apply even to products that launch there well. If you are deciding where to promote your startup, these three limits are the ones to plan around.

1

One launch day, then the day is gone

A Product Hunt launch lives for a single day. The listing stays online afterwards, but the attention is concentrated in those hours: the homepage, the daily ranking and the newsletter all turn over at midnight. A maker who is ill that day, busy that day, or simply in the wrong time zone to answer comments for hours loses the one window the platform is built around. Other boards spread the same launch over several days, a week, or a month-long listing, which gives a product more than one morning to be found. The honest point: a longer window is not a bigger audience — it is a different shape of attention, and for many products it fits better.

2

Attention on the day is winner-takes-most

On any launch day, a handful of products take most of the votes, comments and follow-on coverage, while the rest of the day list is seen by far fewer people. That is how a daily ranking works, and it rewards products that arrive with an audience already gathered. On a quieter board, the same product stands in a shorter line: fewer readers in total, but a real chance to be near the top of the day and to be read properly. Neither shape is fair or unfair — they are just different, and a launch week can use both.

3

There is no real second launch

Product Hunt expects a product to launch once, with a later launch reserved for a product that has changed in a real way. So a weak first listing cannot simply be run again next month with better screenshots. Makers therefore need places to be discovered before the big day — while the product is still a waitlist or a beta — and places to keep being discovered after it, when the spike has passed and the product still needs readers every week. That before-and-after work is exactly what most of the boards below are built for, and it is the gap a one-day platform leaves open by design.

4

Free rarely means instant, anywhere

Almost every board in this guide keeps a free lane open, and almost every free lane has a condition attached: a queue you do not control, a badge or a link you must place on your own site, a review that can say no, or a score your launch must reach to stay listed. None of that makes the free lane worthless — it makes it something to join early and plan around, not something to rely on the night before a launch. Read the catch on each entry below before you count on a date, and treat any board that hides its free route behind an account as a board to check again on the day you submit.

The verified list

8 Product Hunt alternatives, with the real cost of each

Every board below was opened and checked on its own site, and recent launches or listings were visible on each one. Free means a launch route that costs no money today. Freemium means the free route exists but carries a queue, a condition or a limit, with a paid route beside it. Two boards that used to be free and are not any more are named in the box after this list — they are not counted in the 8. If you are also working through the wider free routes, the guide to how to promote your SaaS for free covers what to do with the readers a launch brings.

1

Uneed

A daily launch board where a fresh batch of products goes live each day and the community votes them into daily, weekly and monthly rankings. Cost: freemium — submitting is free and so is the waiting line, but the board assigns your launch date, and it sits well ahead of the day you submit; paid routes buy an earlier date or the right to pick the day yourself. Limit: a free launch has to earn enough support on its day to stay published, and a stronger score is needed for the followed link — so the free lane still asks for a real launch-day effort, just on a date you did not choose.

2

Fazier

A daily product discovery feed with daily, weekly and monthly leaderboards, where new products appear in a steady stream rather than a single ranked rush. Cost: freemium — the free route reviews and lists a product within a stated window of weeks, and paid routes remove conditions and let you pick timing. Limit: the free route asks for a link back to Fazier, placed on your homepage or in your footer, so the free lane is really an exchange — your link for their listing — and a maker who will not place that link has to pay instead.

3

TinyLaunch

A launch board for indie products where launches stay on the homepage for several days and compete for the top places of their launch period. Cost: freemium — the standard launch is free and goes through the standard queue; a paid launch skips the queue. Limit: on the free route, the badge and the high-authority followed link go to the top launches only, and earning one means placing the board badge on your own page — a free launch that finishes mid-table keeps its listing, but not the link prize.

4

Tiny Startups

A curated launch platform and directory for indie and bootstrapped startups, with a weekly leaderboard and a newsletter read by founders. Cost: freemium — a free listing route exists, verified with a public post that mentions the platform; a paid instant listing skips the queue and changes the link to a followed one. Limit: every listing is approved by a person, so a listing takes time rather than appearing on your schedule, the free link is not a followed link, and the audience is founders reading about startups — useful for feedback and peers, less direct for end users of a consumer product.

5

Firsto

A launch platform built around lasting discovery: each launch becomes a permanent product page, with daily, weekly and monthly voting on top. Cost: freemium — a free launch exists, with only a handful of free slots released each day; paid launches go live fast, within a day. Limit: the free queue is very long — the board itself describes a wait measured in months — and the followed link on the free route is reserved for the top launches of the day, with the board badge placed on your site. Join this queue the week you start building, not the week you launch.

6

PeerPush

A product discovery platform built for people and for AI assistants: listings are written in a structured, machine-readable form, so a product can be found by a person browsing and by an assistant answering a question. Cost: freemium — submitting is free and the listing, once live, stays up; a paid standard launch publishes at once or on a date you pick. Limit: free submissions wait in a publishing queue behind paid launches, the product is not visible until its slot arrives, and the board does not publish how long that wait is — fine for steady discovery, hard to plan a dated launch around.

7

Launching Next

A long-running directory that publishes new startups and side projects, with a newsletter on top of the listings. Cost: free to submit — submissions are reviewed every day, and an optional paid upgrade buys consideration within one business day rather than a listing itself. Limit: publication is an editorial decision, not a slot you book: you hear back only if your startup is published, and paying for the faster look does not change that. Treat it as a permanent listing worth having, not a launch event you can schedule a campaign around.

8

StartupBase

A daily launch and discovery platform for SaaS, AI tools and tech startups, where a launch day is followed by a permanent listing across topic pages and collections. Cost: freemium — a free launch joins the queue and publishes in queue order; premium launches, from a one-time fee, let you pick a date within the coming month. Limit: the free route requires the StartupBase badge on your own site, and the board itself says the free wait depends on the line ahead of you — the date is theirs to assign, not yours to choose.

Not counted above: BetaList and Microlaunch. Both were free launch routes for years, and both are still named in older guides as if nothing changed. BetaList now states on its own help pages that all submissions are paid and there is no free submission option; the price appears only at the end of its submission form, and a startup that is not selected is refunded in full. Microlaunch shows only paid launch packs on its public submit pages — opening the submit route leads to the paid offers, starting with its Pro launch — so no free route is offered to a visitor today. Neither is a free route, so neither is in the list of 8. Check their current terms before counting on either, and do not pay for a listing you have not budgeted for.

Match the board to the moment

Which one for which stage

The same board can be the right answer at one stage and the wrong answer at another. This is the order most makers end up using, and it mirrors the longer route in the guide to getting your first 100 users: prepare quietly, launch once in a concentrated way, then keep discovery running after the launch week ends.

1

Pre-launch and waitlist stage: join every slow queue now

While the product is still being built, time is the one thing you have more of than money, and the slow free lanes are priced in time. Join the free queues at Uneed, Firsto and StartupBase, submit to Launching Next for its editorial listing, and put the product on Tiny Startups for founder feedback. None of these needs the product to be finished to be worth starting, and each queue you join months early is a launch asset you did not have to buy later. This is also the stage GrowTogether belongs to: it is a waitlist today, so joining now is how a project is in the circle when the first wave opens, rather than a board you submit to on launch day.

2

Launch week: concentrate on the days you can date

In launch week, timing matters more than the number of boards. Run the Product Hunt day properly — maker present, every comment answered — and place the boards that let a launch run for several days, like TinyLaunch and Fazier, in the same week, so a reader who misses one day meets the product on another. Stagger the rest across the following days instead of firing everything at once: each listing is a separate page, a separate comment thread and a separate chance for someone to write about the product, and spreading them keeps the maker able to answer everywhere. Our guide to promoting with no budget lays out how to run that week without paid help.

3

After launch: steady discovery beats another spike

Once the launch week is over, the need changes from a spike to a stream: permanent pages that keep being found, and people who keep meeting the product in ordinary weeks. The permanent listings — Firsto, PeerPush, Launching Next, StartupBase — do that work quietly, and cross-promotion with neighbouring products does it actively. This is the other place the circle fits: when the first wave opens, discovery inside GrowTogether is not a launch day at all, but members meeting each other’s projects as a habit, which is the shape of attention a launched product actually needs in month two and beyond.

How the list was checked

What “verified” means on this page

Launch boards change their terms often, and old guides keep circulating long after a free lane closes. Every board in the list of 8 was opened on its own site, showed recent launches or listings, and stated its free route or its conditions in its own pages. Boards that could not be confirmed that way were left out rather than listed on trust, and the wider directory of free advertising sites follows the same rule.

1

Read the submit page, not a guide

Guides age in months. The submit page and the help pages are where a board states what it charges today — BetaList says its paid-only rule in its own help pages, in one plain sentence.

2

Find the condition, not just the price

A free lane with a badge, a link back, a queue or a score threshold is still free, but it is not the same offer as a free lane with none of those. The condition decides when and whether the launch happens.

3

Check the date before you plan

If a board assigns the launch date, join early and treat the launch as background. If your campaign needs a fixed day, choose a board that lets you pick it — or accept that timing is the one thing worth paying a single board for.

One more honesty note: no site on this page offers a guarantee of visitors, signups or sales, and neither does any launch board anywhere. A listing is a mechanism — a page, a ranking, a day in front of readers who came to find new products. What those readers do next depends on the product and its page.

Questions people search for

Product Hunt alternatives, answered honestly

What is the best free alternative to Product Hunt?

It depends on your stage. Product Hunt itself is free, and Uneed, Fazier, TinyLaunch, Firsto, PeerPush, Launching Next and StartupBase all offer a free or freemium launch route, while Tiny Startups lists products free with a verification post. Each free route has a catch, usually a long queue, a required badge or link back, or a review that can say no, so the best choice is the one whose catch you can live with.

Are sites like Product Hunt free to launch on?

Some are and some are not. Launching on Product Hunt costs nothing, and several boards in this guide keep a free lane open, but the free lane is rarely instant. BetaList charges for every submission with no free option, and Microlaunch shows only paid packs on its public submit pages, so check the current terms on the site itself before you plan a launch around any board.

Can I launch on Product Hunt more than once?

Product Hunt is built around one launch day for a product, with a new launch expected only when the product has changed in a real way. That is why makers spread launches across other boards before and after the day itself: a waitlist listing while the product is being built, a launch week across several boards, and steady discovery after the spike has passed.

What should I do before launch day on any of these sites?

Prepare the same assets every board asks for: a clear name, a one line description of who the product is for and what it does, a working link, screenshots that show the product in use, and a short first comment from the maker. Then be present on the day itself to answer questions, because a launch page with no maker replies reads as abandoned to everyone who finds it.

Does GrowTogether charge to list a project?

No. There are no plans and no pricing, and the whole platform is free. Members earn credits by discovering projects inside the circle, and their own project enters the rotation to be discovered back.
