The short answer

Fix the message first — one sentence that says who the product is for and what changes for them. Then launch once, properly, on Product Hunt, list the product in the free places where new SaaS is hunted, appear where buyers compare it against the tool it replaces, and show up in the communities and feeds where your users already are.

The free routes — with their limits

Free SaaS promotion, route by route

The routes below cost time, not money — with two named exceptions: two famous listing places in the list below became paid-only in 2026, and their entries say so plainly. Each route also has a ceiling — read it before you spend weeks on the wrong one. One honest note first: no public source publishes a search volume for the exact phrase "how to promote your SaaS for free", so this page makes no volume claim — the demand is real, shown by fresh ranking pages from independent sites, and the routes below are the ones those pages actually recommend.

1

Positioning: one sentence that earns the signup

A SaaS product is sold on its landing page, not in a store: who it is for, what it does, and what changes for them — in one sentence at the top, with a free trial or a demo a visitor can reach in one click. The honest limit: positioning only helps people who already land on your page; it brings no one who has never heard of the product.

2

Launch day: Product Hunt, done properly, once

Product Hunt is where new products are hunted daily: a clear tagline, real screenshots, a short video of the product working, and the maker answering every comment on launch day. The honest limit: you get one real launch day — a weak listing burns it, and a good launch brings a spike of curious visitors, not a permanent stream of users.

3

The 12 places to list your SaaS

Launch boards and directories list new SaaS products by category and keep sending visitors long after you submit — the full list with each place's limit is below. The honest limit: a listing is a signpost, not a crowd; most of these places send a trickle, and the free queue on the popular ones is slow.

4

Alternatives pages: be found by people leaving a rival

Buyers search "alternative to [big tool]" every day. List your product on AlternativeTo and SaaSHub, positioned honestly against the tool it replaces — cheaper, simpler, or built for a narrower job. The honest limit: this only works if your product is a real alternative to something people already search for; a listing with no rival to stand against stays invisible.

5

Communities: Reddit, Indie Hackers and the places your users already gather

Post where your users already are — subreddits for your problem, Indie Hackers, Hacker News Show HN — and lead with the problem you solved, not a signup link. A post that helps first and mentions the product second gets kept; a bare link gets removed. The honest limit: every community has rules against self-promotion, and breaking them once can get you muted there for good.

6

Build in public: X, LinkedIn and integrations

Show the product being built — the bug, the fix, the first user, the number that moved — a little every day, where your users already scroll. And where it fits, integrate with a tool your users already pay for: its directory and its users become a channel you did not have to buy. The honest limit: this is a habit for months, not a launch trick; a week of posts buys almost nothing, and an integration takes real building time.

7

Cross-promotion: other makers are your best channel

Two products with neighbouring audiences recommend each other — inside the product, in a newsletter, in a launch post. The users you gain already pay for this kind of tool. The honest limit: finding partners and keeping it fair by hand does not scale — until a circle runs it for everyone.

8

What never works: bought reviews, fake testimonials, forced signups

Bought reviews are fake accounts — review sites detect them and can remove the listing, and real buyers spot them in seconds. Fake testimonials poison trust with the real users you do reach, and forced signups bring people who churn in a day, which tells every directory your product is not worth showing. Promotion that lasts is pull: real people who chose to sign up.

12 places to list your SaaS

Free SaaS promotion sites, with their limits

Submit to all of them — every one below is free to list on — but read the limit first, so you know what each listing can and cannot do for you. Two famous names are deliberately not in this list: BetaList and Microlaunch both became paid-only in 2026, and their entries in the box below say so plainly.

1

Product Hunt

The biggest launch day for new products. Free to launch; the maker answers comments all day. Limit: one real launch day, and a spike, not a stream.

2

Uneed

A daily launch board with a free queue. Free to launch. Limit: the free queue runs months long, and a launch needs enough upvotes to stay listed — check the current queue before planning around it.

3

Fazier

A launch platform with daily and weekly rankings. Free to launch. Limit: the free route asks for a link back to Fazier on your site; a paid route removes that condition.

4

TinyLaunch

A launch board for new and indie products. Free to launch. Limit: the free queue is slow, and only the top launches get the most visible spots.

5

Tiny Startups

A directory and newsletter for new startups, reviewed by a person. Free to list. Limit: approval is manual, so a listing takes time — and the audience is founders, not end users.

6

AlternativeTo

People searching for an alternative to a big tool find yours here. Free to list. Limit: only helps if your product is a real alternative to something people already search for.

7

SaaSHub

A directory where people compare software by category. Free to list. Limit: a trickle of visitors, not a flood — it compounds over months, and busy categories are hard to stand out in.

8

G2

The largest business-software review site; buyers read it late, when they are ready to choose. Free profile. Limit: a bare profile does nothing — it needs real reviews, roughly ten, before it appears on a category grid.

9

Capterra

Buyers compare business software here before choosing a tool. Free to list. Limit: visibility in busy categories is sold as pay-per-click, so a free listing alone rarely leads the page.

10

Indie Hackers

Founders share products and numbers in public. Free to post. Limit: the audience is builders, so it brings feedback and partners more than end users.

11

Hacker News — Show HN

Show your work to a technical audience that loves new tools. Free to post. Limit: a harsh audience; a post that reads like an ad sinks fast.

12

Reddit — r/SaaS and your niche communities

Subreddits for your problem — r/SaaS, r/SideProject and the community for your niche. Free to post. Limit: strict self-promotion rules; help first, link second.

Not counted above: BetaList and Microlaunch. Both were free launch routes for years. BetaList made submissions a paid service in late 2026, and Microlaunch now offers only a paid launch — so neither is a free route today. They stay named here because founders still search for them; check their current terms before counting on either, and do not pay for a listing you have not budgeted for.

A plan you can actually follow

Your first 100 users in 14 days

This is how to promote my SaaS for free, step by step — and how to reach your first 100 users with no budget and no viral spike: ten real signups a day, from places where people are already looking.

1–2

Days 1–2: fix the message

Rewrite the landing page so one sentence says who the product is for and what changes for them, make the trial or demo reachable in one click, and ask three real users what the product does in one sentence — use their words, not yours.

3–5

Days 3–5: prepare one launch

Prepare the Product Hunt listing — tagline, screenshots, a 30-second video of the product working — and submit to AlternativeTo and SaaSHub the same week, so the listings are live when launch day comes. Join the free queues at Uneed and TinyLaunch now, even though their dates are far out.

6–7

Days 6–7: launch day

Launch, answer every comment yourself, and post the launch in two communities where you have already helped people — not ten places where nobody knows you.

8–14

Days 8–14: build in public, every day

One short post a day about what changed — the fix, the first review, the number that moved — and one honest conversation a day with a user. At ten signups a day, the first 100 arrive before the two weeks are over, and they are users who chose the product.
